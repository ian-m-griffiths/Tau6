## 3. Counter-to / reversal edges

- **K3 "may be less informative (or of higher entropy) than B2"** — the standard ternary simulation logic (used in most HW verification) can be *less* informative than binary in some circuits (¬v∨v evaluates to X); M restores informativeness. Counter-to the common assumption "3-valued ≥ 2-valued always".
- **The dynamic don't-care boundary is the paper's core reversal**: in K3 the care/don't-care split must be fixed in advance; in M it is set *after* each simulation by the output's absolute value (Theorem 3.4) — "the vagueness disappears once the outcome is obtained".
- **M does not always reduce the number of tests**: Cv ≤ Cs is proved, but whether M-tests strictly beat K3-tests in count is left open ("we do not know if it suffices to reduce the number of tests"). Counter-to the natural over-claim that more values ⇒ fewer vectors.
- **0 is deliberately omitted from M** (0 = its own negation, behaves like X) while ±∞ is added — a non-obvious design choice: information comes from *magnitudes*, and 0 carries none.

**RTL-reusable verdict:** the most actionable paper of the batch for `rtl/`. Two concrete adoptions: (1) run `cpu_tb.v` with M-style signed-magnitude vectors (assign distinct absolute values, treat 2'b11 as X, read the output's absolute value as the don't-care boundary) to catch redundant/contradictory logic; (2) the temporal-value trick maps onto our wedge to trace which inputs actually steer a result — a cheap testbench-side provenance checker.

## 5. One-liner: what this paper gives us

A complete theory + two algorithms for verifying digital circuits by simulating with signed-magnitude values (min/max/negation over Ẑ) whose output magnitude marks the dynamic don't-care boundary — directly transferable to our `rtl/` testbench, and its gate algebra is literally our `tand`/`tor`/`tneg` semantics.
